First Author Published Works

The Genetic and Morphological Basis of Local Adaptation to Elevational Extremes in an Alpine Finch

Alpine systems are experiencing rapid environmental change, making it increasingly important to understand local adaptation for conservation, particularly in high-elevation species like the Sierra Nevada Gray-crowned Rosy-Finch. By integrating morphological and genomic analyses, this study found that temperature, food availability, and alpine landscape features impose complex and sometimes conflicting selective pressures on traits linked to thermoregulation and altitude adaptation.

Multinational evaluation of genetic diversity indicators for the Kunming‐Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework

A global assessment across 919 taxa demonstrates that practical genetic diversity indicators can be applied without genomic data and reveals that, while most species retain their populations, over half have populations too small to maintain long-term genetic diversity.

Porcupine/Wntless‐dependent trafficking of the conserved WntA ligand in butterflies

This study shows that the invertebrate-specific WntA signaling protein is evolutionarily conserved, functions through the canonical Wnt secretion pathway, and has been independently lost multiple times across arthropod evolution.

Ongoing Projects

A widespread gap in U.S. Endangered Species Act implementation: Risk of genetic erosion within populations

Genetic diversity is rarely incorporated into biodiversity assessments despite its importance for population viability and evolutionary potential. Applying recently adopted global genetic indicators to 161 species assessed under the U.S. Endangered Species Act revealed that many populations may be at risk of genetic erosion, yet genetic health was not associated with ESA classification status. Incorporating standardized genetic indicators into ESA assessments could improve the early identification and protection of at-risk species.

Loss of Adaptive Capacity Drives Climate Vulnerability Across Taxonomic Scales in an Alpine Specialist Species Complex

Integrating population and landscape genomics with ecological data, this study assessed climate vulnerability and adaptive capacity across the North American Rosy-Finch species complex. The analyses identified a new evolutionarily significant unit, the Sierra Nevada Rosy-Finch, and showed that more isolated lineages have lower adaptive capacity and face greater climate risk. These results demonstrate how incorporating ecological and evolutionary dimensions of adaptive capacity can improve climate vulnerability assessments.

Identifying adaptive variation in spatially structured populations using low-coverage whole-genome sequencing data

This study developed a new genotype–environment association method that accounts for genotype uncertainty in low-coverage sequencing data while explicitly modeling evolutionary processes including local adaptation, migration, mutation, and drift. Applied to synthetic datasets and 3.7 million SNPs from North American rosy-finches, the framework provides a more mechanistic approach for identifying adaptive genetic variation in climate-threatened species.

Counterfactual attribution reveals environmental and niche drivers of non-monotonic century-scale species redistribution under climate change

The Brown-capped Rosy-Finch did not show a simple century-scale upslope shift; instead, its modeled elevational distribution remained broadly stable despite dynamic changes in alpine habitat. Environmental change imposed downslope pressure, particularly through declining summer freezing days at high elevations, but this was partly offset by shifts in species–environment relationships. These counteracting processes demonstrate that apparent distributional stability can mask complex responses to climate change.
