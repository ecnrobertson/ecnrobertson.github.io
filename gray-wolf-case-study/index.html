The story begins

Gray wolves once occupied most of North America.

Over the next several centuries, that range would contract dramatically. Scroll through the story to see how wolves moved from widespread persecution to federal protection and recovery.

Before European settlement

Historical wolf range

For thousands of years, gray wolves occupied an enormous range across North America.

European colonization transformed the continent. Settlements, livestock production, and agriculture expanded westward into wolf habitat.

Wolves were increasingly treated as threats to livestock and human expansion. Governments and private individuals supported intensive eradication campaigns.

Hunting, trapping, poisoning, and bounty programs eliminated wolves from much of the contiguous United States.

By the mid-twentieth century, wolves had disappeared from almost all of their former range in the lower 48 states.

From protection to recovery

Recovery begins

Recovery locations shown schematically

Federal protection shifted the goal from preventing extinction toward recovering viable wolf populations.

In the 1990s, wolves were reintroduced in Yellowstone and central Idaho, accelerating recovery in the Northern Rockies.

Wolf populations grew and expanded beyond the original recovery areas.

Wolves now occupy substantially more of the lower 48 than they did at their mid-twentieth-century low. Recovery success shifted the debate from how to save wolves to where federal protection should end.

Core question

What now?

At this point, the debate is no longer whether or not wolves have recovered anywhere, it is how ESA protection should be applied across different regions.

What counts as a listable population?
What is a “significant portion” of a species’ range?
Are state management plans adequate after delisting?

By the early 2000s, wolf recovery had created a new challenge. The question was no longer only how to protect wolves, but whether federal protections should be reduced in places where recovery goals seemed to be met.

In 2003, FWS tried a broad legal reorganization, creating distinct population segments and reducing protections for some populations. Courts rejected that first attempt, showing that the legal geography of wolf recovery would be heavily contested.

FWS tried again in 2007 and 2008, delisting wolves in the Western Great Lakes and Northern Rocky Mountains. Litigation followed almost immediately, and protections were restored as courts reviewed the agency’s logic and boundaries.

The story then became even more tangled. Congress intervened in the Northern Rockies, and Wyoming’s status bounced back and forth as courts weighed whether state management was strong enough to sustain recovery after federal protections were removed.

By 2020, FWS moved to delist gray wolves across the lower 48 except the Mexican wolf. At this stage, the central debate had shifted: if wolves are recovered in some places but not others, where should federal protection end?
